Learning to Love the Imperfect

One of the greatest traits of humans is our ability to build. To take disparate things and combine them into novel results. We do this to solve problems and make our lives easier, or we do this just for the joy of creation; but no matter what pursuit we engage in as humans, whether it is art, woodworking, or even rocket surgery, we inevitably encounter the external pressure to be better.

Take a child, for example. Well, don’t literally take one. That’s illegal. Instead, let’s think of a fictional child. That’s not illegal (I think, I’m not a lawyer). Children simply… create. They don’t judge what they’ve created because what do they have to compare it to? A child doesn’t care about Rembrandt or Beethoven or Grace Hopper.

A child, with wild abandon, will scribble on a page or mash the keys on a piano and call it good. They’ll blunder and stumble and ram their way through and think themselves good at it. It’s something my violin instructor is quite fond of reminding me. Older students are a blessing to have because we are aware of when or even how we make mistakes, while younger students are oblivious to those very mistakes. Older students are a curse to have because we are aware of when or even how we make mistakes, while younger students are oblivious to those very mistakes.

It is when we enter school is that we start to feel the judgement of others upon our shoulders. We are taught that there is a right way and a wrong way to do things. We are told that we have failed in the creation of our art because our teachers did not approve of it, no matter how much fun we had in the creation.

Indeed, I remember one such rebuke from second grade. I don’t remember what the task was, but I remember that I was drawing a pig that was sad. And because that pig was sad he was crying. And because he was crying, he caused a flood. And because everything was supposed to be flooded, the page needed to be blue.

To my mind, this made perfect sense. But to my teacher, I was scribbling and thus doing the task wrong. I can’t help but think that this is one of the consequences of how we’ve structured schooling in the modern age. With the introduction of standardized testing, schools are very much focused on the right and wrong way of doing things. From our earliest days of school we are judged and assessed. When we make mistakes we are expected to erase them and redo it better; or we simply fail and are told that was our only chance. If you don’t do it right the first time, then you’re shamed for your failure… and then you move on with the next lesson.

As we age, we begin to interanlize these ideals until our inner voice sounds less like a creator and more like a proctor wielding a switch.

So. What can we do? When I was stationed in Yokosuka, Japan I learned about two things. One you may have heard of. The other you likely haven’t, unless you’ve spent some time over there outside of tourism. The one you’ve likely heard of, even if you don’t know the exact name, is Kintsugi.

Kintsugi translates literally into kin = gold, tsugi = joinery. Gold joinery is a form of repair most commonly employed with broken pottery or ceramics. If a beloved cup or teapot breaks, then a lacquer mixed with gold powder is used to join the broken pieces again. This creates a beautiful network of golden seams.

The other concept which is much less known outside of Japan is wabi-sabi. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, wabi may be translated as “subdued, austere beauty”, and sabi as “rustic patina.” It is often described as accepting and finding beauty in the imperfect, transient and incomplete.

How does this all relate to what we make? Wabi-sabi belongs at the beginning of our creations, quietly weaving through the process, and giving a gentle wave at the end. It acknowledges that wobbly lines are okay. It asks us to stop treating perfection as the only qualification for something to have value. Wabi-sabi tells us that a flaw does not necessarily ruin our work; sometimes, it is precisely the flaw that allows a thing to become more itself. Ask a machine to create a hundred tables and you probably won’t get a response. But if you build a machine to make those tables, you’ll get a hundred virtually identical tables. Ask a human to create a hundred tables and after you convince them you really do need a hundred tables, you get a hundred similar tables. But each one will have its own flaws that make it unique from the others. This is wabi-sabi can be found. In the variations of material, circumstances or just the mood of the creator that day.

Kintsugi waits until disaster strikes. When something goes wrong we step back and ask if that flaw can become a part of the creation’s identity. It is the conscious decision to visibly repair damage. A poorly joined seam on a pair of pants can become a pocket. An accidental note in a piece of music could turn into a personal flourish before returning to the original piece.

These two concepts together do not tell us to purposefully seek failure. A broken teapot is still a broken teapot. An improperly joined chair will likely collapse the first time it’s sat on. Rather, they tell us that what would normally be considered a fatal mistake could instead become a part of the creation. If something truly needs to be remade, then we remake it. But when a mistake doesn’t compromise the structure or safety of the work, it can become evidence that the object passed through human hands; the hands of a messy, endlessly adaptable, beautiful human that made something that would not have otherwise existed in quite the same way.
